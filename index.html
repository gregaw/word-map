    How it works

    Every word is translated into 43 languages. The page then measures how alike the words sound and colours the map from that.

    How alike two words sound

    1. Romanise Cyrillic, Greek, Georgian and Armenian into Latin letters (слово → slovo).
    2. Tidy up: drop articles (das Wort → Wort) and accents.
    3. Spell by sound: Polish sz, Czech š and German sch all become sh; w becomes v, ph becomes f, and so on.
    4. Compare in two ways and keep the higher score:
      • Sound classes. Sounds that swap as a word travels between languages form a class: t/d, k/g, p/b/f, s/z/sh, cz/ch/ts, and so on. Turning one word into the other costs little within a class, least for vowels (they drift most), and most for adding or dropping a consonant. The score is 1 minus that cost, divided by the cost of the whole longer word. This follows Dolgopolsky's method for spotting related words.
      • Consonant skeleton. The consonants in order survive when a language drops a syllable: czwartek is cz-w-r-t-k and четвер is č-t-v-r. This counts only when two words share at least consonants in order, and then for at most .

    How the groups are found

    Average-linkage clustering. Every language starts as a group of its own. The two groups whose words are most alike on average are merged: every word in one is compared with every word in the other. This repeats until no two groups average or more. A language left on its own stays grey.

    Why the average, and not just any close pair? Chains of near neighbours would join whole families that are not alike: the Slavic czwartek and the Germanic torsdag are linked through forms in between.

    Groups by origin

    For Thursday, Saturday and Christmas, history matters more than sound. The Latin jeudi, giovedì and jueves (all "Jupiter's day") have drifted too far apart for any sound measure to group them without also merging the Slavic and Germanic groups. So their groups are set by hand from the words' origins and named in the side panel: "Thor's day", "Jupiter's day", "the fourth day" and so on.
